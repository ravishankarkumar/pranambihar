Bhuiya Baba Puja is not one uniform ceremony. Names such as Bhuiya Baba, Bhuiyan Baba, Bhuiyan Puja, Bhumi Puja and Gahabar Puja appear in different places and accounts, with meanings shaped by the community, shrine and landscape in which the tradition is practised.

## Land, labour and ancestors

Research on ritual performance in Bihar describes Bhuiyan or Bhumi Puja as worship connected with land, labour and an ancestral presence. The same event may be named through its deity, shrine, performer, drum or mode of performance. That variety is important: it cautions against turning many local practices into a single supposedly definitive ritual.

In some accounts, Bhuiya Baba is understood as a guardian associated with a village and its land. Shrines may be non-iconic or may contain a figure, and their placement and form differ. A documented folklore account from Mithila describes Bhuyan than sites beneath a large peepal tree or near a pond, while also recording substantial variation between two observed shrines.

## Pastoral life and milk offerings

Bhuiya Baba worship has a particularly visible relationship with cattle-rearing communities in parts of north Bihar. A 2023 report from Basaith in Madhubani describes farmers and pastoralists arriving from Madhubani, Darbhanga, Sitamarhi and neighbouring Nepal to offer milk after making vows concerning the health or reproduction of their cattle.

At that shrine, devotees commonly arrive on Mondays and Fridays carrying milk in a lota or container. The report records the temple priest and worshippers explaining the offering through care for cows, buffaloes and other livestock. This is one documented regional practice, not a template for every Bhuiya Baba tradition.

## A tradition that is also performed

Song and performance form another strand. Sangeet Natak Akademi’s 2016–17 annual report records a **Bhuiyan Baba Gayan** performance by Tej Narayan Choudhari and a group from Madhubani at a national festival of folk and traditional expression.

Other research discusses drums, singing, narration and spirit-directed performance under several overlapping local names. A future Pranam Bihar field report should therefore listen not only for a ritual sequence, but also for the songs, instruments, performers and community relationships through which the tradition becomes visible.

## What still needs to be documented

The puja’s calendar, offerings, food and sequence can change between villages and families. Oral notes collected for this atlas mention kheer cooked in an earthen pot in connection with Bhuiya Baba worship in at least one household tradition. We are retaining that as a lead for firsthand reporting rather than presenting it as a practice shared everywhere.

This page is a researched beginning. Its next stage should be built from conversations with worshippers, pastoralists, bhagats, singers, priests and families in more than one district.
