A viewer may first recognise a Mithila painting by its filled surface, double-line borders, plants, animals and figures held in a dense visual rhythm. Recognition is useful. It can also flatten difference.

“Madhubani art” is often presented as though one anonymous tradition produces every image. In reality, artists inherit, choose, alter and sign. Subject, line, colour, material and market have changed across generations. A painting made for a wall, one made on paper for sale and one created in response to a contemporary event do not have identical lives.

## Begin with the artist

Pranam Bihar’s coverage will name makers wherever authorship is known. We will ask who taught them, how they prepare a surface, where materials come from, what a motif means to them and how buyers influence the work. We will also distinguish careful adaptation from the casual extraction of a recognisable border onto any available product.

This does not require freezing the form. Living traditions move. Artists experiment with scale, new subjects and new ways of earning. The important question is whether they retain authorship, credit and a meaningful share of the value created from their work.

## A visual archive with voices

The future collection will pair close photographs of process with recorded or written artist accounts. Translations will be credited. Reproductions will be used with permission. Where an image has entered a museum or private collection, its path will be part of the story.

The border is not the edge of the subject. It is where a closer reading begins.
