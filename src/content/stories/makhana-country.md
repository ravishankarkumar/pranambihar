Makhana is often introduced through a bowl: pale, crisp and ready to season. In northern Bihar, the story begins much earlier—in ponds and low-lying fields, with seed, mud, water and highly specialised labour.

Government and industry studies describe Bihar as the centre of Indian makhana production, particularly across the districts of Mithila and Seemanchal. The scale matters, but percentages alone cannot explain the crop. Its value depends on people who understand aquatic cultivation, harvest difficult seed and transform it through heat and precise manual work.

## An ingredient is also a system

Calling makhana a “superfood” may help sell a packet, but it hides the more interesting questions. Who carries the risk when water conditions change? Where does processing happen? How much value remains near the place of cultivation? What happens when a ceremonial or fasting food becomes an everyday branded snack?

Those questions connect ecology with enterprise. Improved varieties and field-based cultivation are changing production. New companies are changing packaging and distribution. A geographical indication for Mithila Makhana creates another opportunity—but also makes careful use of regional identity more important.

## What Pranam Bihar will follow

This will become a reported series rather than a single celebratory article. We will follow the crop from cultivation and popping to local markets and national shelves. Farmers, processors, researchers and cooks will each see a different part of the system.

The purpose is not to claim an old food as a newly discovered trend. It is to make visible the knowledge and labour that were present before the trend arrived—and to ask what a more valuable future could look like for the region that sustains it.
